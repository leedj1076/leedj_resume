## Section 3: Career Transitions & Intentionality

### Q3.1: You started as an engineer — what made you switch to business?

It wasn't a conscious switch until it became necessary. At TmaxTibero, I was pure engineer. That was my identity. But even there, I noticed something: the engineers who got the best outcomes weren't the ones who just solved the technical problem perfectly. They were the ones who understood why the customer cared and could translate that back into the problem statement. That started shifting my mind.

At Flint, I became co-founder-COO almost accidentally. Three of us were technical co-founders, and someone had to think about sales, marketing, fundraising, and operations. I volunteered because I knew the product better than anyone, and I thought I could sell what I understood deeply. That was the crucible moment. I realized I loved the problem-solving of business more than I loved the problem-solving of pure engineering.

Here's what I mean: in engineering, you have a problem, you build a solution, you measure if it works. It's very clean. In business, the problem is messier — you have to figure out what problem actually matters to customers, then you have to solve it, then you have to convince them to pay for it, then you have to scale it. It's an order of magnitude harder. But it's also more leveraged. One good business decision can affect hundreds of thousands of people. One good engineering decision affects the quality of their experience.

I didn't switch away from engineering — I expanded my toolkit. At DUG, I built the support system myself. I think in code. But I optimize for business impact, not technical elegance. That's the evolution. The switch wasn't "I'm done with engineering." The switch was "Engineering is a tool I use to solve business problems, not the end state itself."

### Q3.2: What did you learn at TmaxTibero?

Three deep lessons that shaped everything after.

**First: enterprise problems are unglamorous but massive.** TmaxTibero was doing DBMS migration for Samsung — not a sexy problem. No viral growth, no consumer narrative. But Samsung's infrastructure is used by millions of people, and if that DBMS migration fails, entire supply chains break. That taught me that the most important problems are often invisible to consumers. They're infrastructure problems. That perspective changed how I evaluate startups now.

**Second: reliability and edge cases matter more than perfection on the happy path.** In enterprise systems, you're not optimizing for 95% of cases. You're optimizing for the 1% that breaks. You're thinking about what happens if a server fails at 3am on a Sunday, or if someone tries to run a query that should never be run but will be. That paranoia, that attention to edge cases — that's what separates systems that actually scale from systems that work in demos.

**Third: I learned that I'm capable of depth.** I dove deep into DBMS architecture, query optimization, distributed systems. I proved to myself that I could master complex technical domains. That confidence was huge. Later, when I was a founder and people would ask "can you figure out GNNs?" I knew the answer was yes, because I'd done it before.

The meta-lesson: TmaxTibero taught me that I could operate in unglamorous, complex technical environments and add value. That's actually a superpower in VC, because most investors are chasing sexy narratives. I can smell when an "unglamorous" problem is actually a massive market opportunity.

### Q3.3: What happened to Flint Technologies? Why did you leave?

We shut down, and I take responsibility for my part in that. Here's the brutal honest version:

We built a GNN-based knowledge graph platform. The vision was beautiful: any organization could map their internal knowledge, connections, and insights. We raised $212K at $2.127M post-money, which meant investors believed in us. We got 860 users in seven months, which sounds successful on paper.

But we had a UX problem we didn't solve early enough. The core action was manual — users had to manually connect nodes to build their knowledge graph. That sounds simple, but it wasn't scalable in practice. It broke down when knowledge graphs got more than 500 nodes. And we were too optimistic that "power users will love this." They didn't. It was friction at the core of the product.

I, as COO, should have caught this earlier and made the hard call: either we build auto-connection features, or we pivot the model completely. Instead, we tried to push through. Then we saw some early customer interest from insurance companies and thought "maybe the B2B insurance play is it." We pivoted hard, changed our narrative entirely, and lost product-market fit. By the time we realized the insurance unit economics didn't work, we were burned out and out of capital.

Why did I leave? I didn't "leave" — the company shut down. But if it hadn't, I probably would have stepped down. I was emotionally exhausted, I knew the fundamental problem hadn't been solved, and I didn't want to spend two more years chasing a solution I didn't believe in. 

The learning: **ship fast, but solve core friction fast.** Don't optimize for 800 users if your core model breaks at 500. Make the hard product decision early. I was too conflict-averse as a young founder. I should have forced that decision between my co-founders.

## Section 4: Startup Experience & Founder Empathy

### Q4.1: What's the most important thing when starting a company?

**Founder chemistry.** Not vision, not market size, not funding. Those are all table stakes. The thing that actually determines if you survive the first two years is whether you can work with your co-founders when everything is hard.

At Flint, I had three brilliant co-founders. We had complementary skills, we raised funding, we built a product people used. But we didn't have the kind of communication norms that let us make hard decisions together quickly. When the UX friction problem emerged, we couldn't have the conversation "this is a fundamental product flaw and we need to fix it radically" without it becoming emotional. We should have been able to say "the model as stated doesn't work, here are three options, which one do we commit to?" Instead, it became personality and ego.

I've seen this at DUG and in founders I know: the ones who win are the ones who can have clear-eyed, low-ego conversations about hard problems. They disagree, they commit, they move forward. The ones who fail are the ones where one founder is secretly checking out, or where there's unspoken resentment, or where criticism gets taken personally instead of professionally.

That's not something you can teach. It's something you have to build in. And it's something you can lose. The most important thing is: can you tell your co-founder "I think you're making a mistake and here's why" without that becoming a referendum on your relationship? If the answer is no, the company is doomed eventually.

Everything else — product, market, capital — you can fix. You can't fix bad founder chemistry. That's the thing I'd optimize for if I were starting again.

### Q4.2: What's the difference between a good investor and a bad investor?

**Good investor:** You call them at 2am because your key customer is leaving and you need help. They don't tell you "that's your problem to solve." They say "okay, here's what I'm thinking" and either they help you debug it, or they connect you to someone who can. They ask you hard questions: "Have you actually talked to the customer about why they're leaving?" They push you to solve root cause, not symptoms. They also understand their own constraints — they know which decisions they can help with and which they can't. They're useful.

**Bad investor:** They show up when it's good news. They disappear when it's hard. They give generic advice that applies to nothing. They're worried about their own returns first and your company second. They make decisions based on optics instead of fundamentals — they'd rather you be "the next Uber" than actually profitable. They don't do the work to understand your market deeply. They treat the company as a line item on their spreadsheet instead of a thing they're actually invested in.

The difference is: good investors are useful operators. Bad investors are expensive capital.

From Flint, I learned something specific: there are investors who will push back on your pitch and make you stronger, and investors who just write a check. We got support from Strong Ventures and Fast Ventures that was genuinely valuable — they pushed us on metrics, on competitive positioning. That was more useful than investors who just said "cool idea, here's $500K." When you're a founder, you're already second-guessing yourself 100 times a day. You need investors who help you think, not investors who just say yes.

I also got a rejection from Meta's fund on the merits, and that was actually better than a yes based on weaker analysis. Because the rejection was explicit: "We looked at your financials, we looked at your market size, we looked at your retention, and we don't think this is a category-level opportunity." That's feedback I could actually use.

### Q4.3: If you could restart Flint, what would you do differently?

Three things, in order of impact.

**First: ruthlessly prioritize the core UX friction.** The moment we saw that retention dropped off with >500 nodes, that should have been a all-hands-on-deck problem. Not in three months, not in a quarter — immediately. We should have said: "Everything else stops. We are solving auto-connection. Do we build it? Do we build templated knowledge graphs? Do we change the model entirely?" We should have made that decision in two weeks, not two months.

**Second: be more disciplined about when to pivot.** We saw some early traction in insurance, and we thought "maybe this is it!" Insurance is a $1T+ market, of course it's attractive. But we didn't ask: "Do we actually understand insurance? Do we have an unfair advantage in insurance? Or are we just chasing a big market because our original market isn't working?" We should have been ruthless: either we solve the core product problem, or we have a radically new insight about a different market. We didn't have that insight.

**Third: I'd set a hard decision point earlier.** Something like: "In six months, we need to hit these metrics on retention and NPS. If we don't, we're either going to kill the company, find a buyer, or radically pivot. We will not be a zombie." We were a zombie for several months — not fully committed to anything. That's the worst state. If I restarted, I'd set that guardrail: make progress on core metrics or make a hard decision.

The meta-lesson: **clarity and decisiveness early beats optimism and muddiness later.** I was optimistic we'd figure it out. I should have been clear about what "figured it out" actually meant.

### Q4.4: What was hardest about fundraising as a founder?

**Emotionally:** The rejection. You pour everything into a 10-minute pitch, you get a "no" from someone you really respected, and you go home thinking "did they say no because the idea is bad or because I pitched it poorly?" That ambiguity is brutal. You second-guess yourself constantly.

**Intellectually:** Having to code-switch between founder and salesperson. When I was pitching, I had to be confident, visionary, and certain. But I'm also naturally cautious and probabilistic. I see all the ways something can fail. Founders aren't supposed to sound like that. So I'd spend 10 minutes being "the guy who's going to own this market" and then my co-founder would see me the next day going "actually, I'm worried about X, Y, Z." That cognitive dissonance is exhausting.

**Practically:** The information asymmetry. Investors see hundreds of pitches. They know patterns I don't. I don't know if they said "no" because they have a better company in that category already, or because they think the market doesn't exist. They're not obligated to tell me. So I'm fundraising half-blind. I'm optimizing for something I can't fully see.

But the hardest thing overall: **I was asking people for money while simultaneously needing them to believe in me.** That's a weird psychological position. If I had been 100% confident, it would have been easier. If I had been 0% confident, I could have just asked for advice. But at 60% confident — which is probably where founders should be — you're in this weird space where you're vulnerable and also selling.

That's why I now know: good investors give founders the safety to be honest about uncertainty. Bad investors force founders to hide it.

### Q4.5: How did you feel emotionally when Flint shut down?

Honestly? A lot of things.

**Relief:** Which sounds weird, but it's true. We had been struggling for six months. Everyone was tired. I knew in my gut that the fundamental product problem hadn't been solved, and we were burning capital and energy trying to pretend it had. Shutting down was like finally admitting something I already knew. There was relief in that clarity.

**Shame:** Not shame about failing — I'm intellectually clear that failure is part of the game. But I felt shame about the people I'd brought into it. I had convinced my co-founders, I had convinced customers, I had convinced investors. And it didn't work out. That's on me. I had to sit with "I led people down a path that didn't work" and that was uncomfortable.

**Humility:** I thought I knew more than I did. I thought "we're founding this company, we're going to be able to figure it out." Turns out, there are things you can't figure out if you've never done them before. I didn't know how to identify a fundamental product problem vs. a temporary growth issue. I didn't know how to make hard decisions as a co-founder. Failure taught me I was more ignorant than I thought. That's valuable but it was hard to sit with.

**Determination:** Pretty quickly — like a week after shutdown — I felt a strong pull to figure out what I actually learned and make sure it counted for something. I didn't want to be a founder who failed and then moved on. I wanted to be a founder who failed and then used that to understand how other founders could succeed. That feeling drove me to DUG and now to VC.

I don't think I'm special for feeling this mix. Most founders feel relief, shame, humility, and determination when they fail. The difference is what you do with those feelings.

Not in the next 5-7 years. That's my honest timeline.

Here's why: I have a theory I want to test first. My theory is that I'm more valuable as a multiplier — helping 20 companies think clearly about strategy — than as a single founder building one company. Flint disproved the idea that I should be a solo founder without experienced investors. DUG showed me I can execute operationally. But neither of those proved I should start another company right now.

I want to spend 5-7 years in VC, see 100+ companies up close, understand what patterns actually matter, build credibility with the ecosystem. Then, if I see a problem I want to solve and I have co-founders I'd trust with my life, then maybe I'd start something again.

But here's the thing: I'm not saying "never." I'm saying "not now." If in 3 years I see a AI + knowledge management problem that I can't NOT solve, and I find two other people I trust completely, I might leave Altos. But that would be a conscious choice based on genuine insight, not an itch I can't scratch.

As a founder, I learned that starting a company should be something you're called to do, not something you default to because you're restless. Right now, I'm not called to do it. I'm called to learn and help others win. If that changes, everyone will know because I'll be clear about it.

## SECTION 14: Technical Depth & AI

### Q14.1: Explain RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation).

RAG is solving a core problem: language models are smart but they're not current, they don't have proprietary knowledge, and they hallucinate. RAG is the practical fix.

Here's how it works: Instead of asking the model to generate everything from its training data, you first retrieve relevant context from your data, then pass both that context AND the user's question to the model. The model generates based on grounded information, not just its parameters.

Simple example: You ask a customer service chatbot "What's your return policy?" Without RAG, the model generates plausible-sounding nonsense. With RAG, you:
1. Retrieve your actual return policy docs
2. Pass those docs + the question to the model
3. Get an accurate answer based on your real policy

Why it matters for business: You get accuracy (fewer hallucinations), you can update knowledge by just updating your documents (no retraining), and you can restrict context to what users should know (privacy, security).

At DUG, we built a CS RAG system that retrieves relevant game mechanics, player issues, and solutions before generating support responses. This replaced ticket routing to humans for common issues. It reduced customer service latency by 60% while improving accuracy because the model had guardrails from our curated knowledge base.

The limitations: RAG is only as good as your retrieval. If your retriever can't find the relevant doc, the model won't help. It's also not solving the fundamental problem that models are statistical systems. For safety-critical decisions, RAG helps but isn't sufficient—you still need human review.

And RAG is computationally expensive. You're doing retrieval + generation, which costs money per query. So you need to be strategic about when to use it versus cheaper patterns.

Investing angle: Companies claiming RAG as competitive advantage need scrutiny. RAG is becoming infrastructure (Pinecone, Weaviate). The differentiation is in domain knowledge and retrieval quality, not the RAG pattern itself.

### Q14.2: What's the most important thing to watch when investing in AI?

Unit economics of the AI feature, not unit economics of the business.

Most AI startups I see confuse these. They say: "Our CAC is $50 and LTV is $500, so we're fine." But if the AI feature costs $30 per customer per month to run (inference costs, GPU, etc.), and customers only pay $20 per month for that feature, you're underwater. The business model works until it doesn't.

At Flint, we didn't watch this closely enough. Our GNN model required infrastructure cost that didn't justify the pricing we could charge. The users who loved the product couldn't afford the feature-specific cost. Scaling looked good on topline growth because we weren't tracking cost of serving each user's AI requests.

So when I evaluate AI companies now, I ask:
1. What's the inference cost per customer per month? (LLM calls, GPU inference, embedding storage—actual dollars)
2. What can you charge for that? (Pricing power for AI features is lower than we think)
3. What's the gross margin on the AI feature itself?

If inference cost is $5 and feature revenue is $10, you're at 50% margins on the feature. That's viable. If inference cost is $15 and feature revenue is $20, you're weaker—vulnerable to pricing pressure or inference cost increases.

Second thing: ask how defensible the AI is. Is it:
- Proprietary data (strong—harder to replicate)?
- Proprietary model (weak—models are becoming commodities)?
- Better prompting/RAG (weak—also becoming commodities)?
- Proprietary system that's better because of data + model + UX together (strong)?

Flint was model-focused ("we have a better GNN"). That's not defensible. DUG is system-focused ("we have the best XR game engine because of our data pipeline + model + 3D understanding"). That's more defensible.

Third: Can they show unit economics improving? If CAC is high because they're early, but the AI system learns and costs drop, that's a path. If costs are flat and are structurally high, that's a ceiling.

### Q14.4: Explain GNN to a non-technical person.

A neural network is basically a system that learns patterns from data. A Graph Neural Network is optimized for data that has relationships.

Here's a concrete example: Imagine you're at a social network like LinkedIn. You want to recommend a job to someone. A regular neural network would look at that person's profile (education, experience, location) and guess what jobs they'd like. But that misses something important: what are jobs that people *similar to them* took? What do people in their network do?

A GNN explicitly looks at the graph—the web of connections between people and jobs and companies. It learns not just from individual features but from patterns in the network itself. "People in this role who went to MIT tend to move to startups. This person went to MIT, is in similar role, so startups might interest them."

Why does this matter? For many real-world problems, the relationships matter as much as the individual things. Recommendation systems, fraud detection (is this transaction similar to fraud patterns in your network?), drug discovery (molecules are graphs), supply chains (all relationships).

At Flint, our pitch was: GNNs are the future for recommendation problems. And they probably are, eventually. But we didn't have the right problem. We were trying to force GNN sophistication into a problem that didn't actually require it. A simpler model would have worked fine and been faster.

That's a lesson: just because you have sophisticated technology doesn't mean every problem needs it. GNNs are powerful and defensible when the problem is genuinely about relationships and patterns. When it's not, you're overcomplicating.

### Q14.6: Can AI replace VC work?

Not in the near term. Probably not even in the medium term.

Here's what AI is good at: finding patterns at scale. If you can encode the question "which companies have metrics similar to successful exits in this category," AI can do it better than humans. Pattern matching on data is going to get better.

But VC work isn't pattern matching on data. It's:
1. Judgment under uncertainty—betting on founders and visions that aren't proven
2. Building conviction from asymmetric information—I might know something about the market that doesn't show up in metrics
3. Advising through inflection points—when everything breaks, what's the right call?
4. Fundraising and board management—relationships matter
5. Industry context—understanding how this trend in AI affects that market in fintech

An AI system trained on historical VC data will find the next obvious pattern. It won't find the non-obvious bet. It will optimize for past success, which won't predict future success if the world is changing.

What AI *will* do: eat away at mechanical parts of VC work. Due diligence on financials? AI can flag anomalies better. Competitive analysis? AI can surface competitive risk faster. Market sizing? AI can draft frameworks.

So the AI risk for VCs isn't replacement. It's that the mechanical parts get automated, and you have to be better at the judgment parts.

For Altos specifically: you have brand, founder networks, industry context, and relationship depth. AI accelerates mechanical work. It makes fast analysis of opportunities possible at scale. But it won't replace the conviction-building, the advising, the relationships that actually drive returns.

If I join, I'm thinking: how do I use AI to do better pattern detection on opportunities, faster due diligence on founders and markets, but then spend my best energy on things AI can't do—genuine advising, founder development, conviction-building.

### Q15.3: How did you achieve 55.47% YoY growth?

### Q15.4: Describe your investment evaluation framework.

I start with founder-market fit, not founder quality or market size separately.

Founder quality alone doesn't mean much—great people can bet on bad markets. Market size alone doesn't mean much—huge markets with weak founders fail. I'm looking for: does this founder understand this specific market deeply, have conviction they're right, and have a plan to win even if they're wrong?

Quick framework:

**Founder conviction (35%)**
- How long have they thought about this problem? (Hobby → years of thought → lived experience)
- Do they have skin in the game? (Customer problem or founding for impact?)
- What would change their mind? (Rigid conviction is dangerous; flexible conviction is good)
- Can they articulate why existing solutions are insufficient? (Not just criticism—specific gaps)

**Market timing (25%)**
- Are they early or late? (Early → you're funding their learning; late → you're funding execution)
- What changed recently that makes now the right time? (Regulatory shift, technology maturity, user behavior)
- What's the TAM? (Not market size—TAM they could realistically address given their approach)
- What's the TAM growing toward? (Biggest opportunity is often adjacent, not in the pitch)

**Business model (20%)**
- Can they explain exactly how they make money? (Unit economics, CAC, LTV, churn, not vague "B2B SaaS")
- What's the moat? (Why can't someone else do this in 2 years?)
- What's the path to 10x+ returns? (I'm not funding lifestyle businesses; what's the venture-scale outcome?)

**Execution capability (15%)**
- Can they articulate their next 12-month milestones?
- Do they know what they don't know? (Founders who claim to know everything are red flags)
- Do they have a playbook from similar successes? (Or pattern-match to why it failed elsewhere)
- Is the team composition right for this phase? (Early: scrappy problem solvers; growth: domain expertise)

I also pay attention to:
- **Investor fit**: Do I believe them when they say they want my help? Do they want board reports or actual advising?
- **Founder mental health**: Do they seem sustainable? Flint taught me that burnout founders make poor decisions late-stage.

What I deliberately don't weight:
- Pedigree (good schools help, not decisive)
- Network effects (everyone claims them, rarely real)
- First-mover advantage (usually not relevant)

I also do negative screening. I pass on:
- Founders I don't trust (integrity matters more than talent)
- Markets that feel faddish (crypto, AI hype without fundamentals)
- Business models with structural unit economics problems
- Teams with obvious skill gaps that they're not addressing

At Flint, I missed the execution capability piece—we were model-obsessed but didn't have product and UX expertise. At DUG, I weighted founder conviction heavily and it was right—my conviction that XR gaming was real, plus team capability to execute in a hard market.

### Q15.6: What are the key metrics for a business model?

Depends on the model, but I look for:

**For SaaS (B2B or B2C):**
- CAC payback period (how many months to recover customer acquisition cost)
- LTV:CAC ratio (I want 3:1 minimum, 5:1 good)
- Churn rate (monthly churn <5% is good; >10% is warning)
- NRR (net revenue retention—are existing customers growing or shrinking? >100% is great)
- Gross margin (>70% is healthy; <50% is concerning for unit economics)

I also look at the *composition* of these metrics:
- Is CAC growing or shrinking? (Bad if growing—means marketing is getting weaker)
- Is churn consistent or seasonal?
- Is NRR coming from expansion or retention? (Retention is more sustainable)

**For marketplaces:**
- Take rate (what % of transaction value do you keep?)
- Frequency of transactions per user per period
- Supply quality and retention (are suppliers happy? How fast is churn?)
- Unit economics of a single transaction (does it make sense at scale?)

**For content/games (like DUG):**
- Cohort retention curves (does each new cohort retain better than the last? That's a sign of getting better)
- LTV per download, not just per paying user
- Payback period on content creation (if it costs $100k to make a game, how long to recoup?)
- Virality coefficient (how many new users does each user bring?)

**For AI/infrastructure:**
- Inference cost per unit of output (critical for AI)
- Customer acquisition cost vs. unit economics of serving that customer
- Do you have a path to margin improvement? (Inference gets cheaper? Automation scales?)

**Universal metrics I always check:**
- Burn rate vs. runway (how long until you run out of money?)
- Customer concentration (are you 30% dependent on one customer? Red flag)
- Repeat revenue percentage (what % of revenue is recurring?)

What I don't care about until it matters:
- Vanity metrics (total users, total revenue). Cohort metrics matter.
- "Growth rate" without context (2x growth on $100K is different from 1.5x on $10M)

At Flint, we tracked users and feature engagement, but we didn't track CAC or unit economics carefully. That was a major miss. At DUG, we track 30+ metrics religiously, which is too many. But better to be granular and then aggregate.

The principle: pick 3-5 metrics that matter for your business model, understand them deeply, and use them to make decisions.

### Q15.7: How do you quickly figure out what the other party wants?

Ask directly, then listen more than you talk.

- If they correct me, I listen to the correction, not to myself.
- I repeat back: "So you're really trying to solve X, and Y is secondary?"

In hiring (and board relationships), I do this constantly:
- "What are you actually worried about in this role?"
- "When you imagine success in 18 months, what does that look like?"
- "What do you need from me?"

People will answer these questions if you actually want the answer. Most people don't ask—they assume they know. I've been wrong enough times that I've learned to ask.

Technical tip: I take notes during conversations. Knowing I'm serious about understanding makes people more candid. And I review my notes and send back: "Here's what I heard—did I miss anything?" That closes the loop.

Weakness: I can do this too much, which makes people uncomfortable. Not every conversation needs deep clarity. I've had to learn when to just make a decision rather than over-analyze.

## SECTION 18: VC Finance & Deal Structure

### Q18.1: Explain pre-money vs post-money valuation and why it matters.

**Pre-money** is what the company is worth before you invest. **Post-money** is what it's worth after your check clears.

Simple math: If I'm investing $1M and the pre-money is $4M, then post-money = $5M. If I'm investing $1M at a $5M post-money, then pre-money = $4M. They're linked, but the direction matters for ownership.

Why this matters:

**For founders:** Pre-money is what you negotiate. If you're raising $1M, saying "pre-money $4M" feels better than "post-money $5M" even though they're identical. Psychologically, founders prefer to argue about pre-money because it feels smaller. Smart founders always quote pre-money.

**For investors:** Post-money matters for dilution calculation. If there's another follow-on round coming, I care about post-money because that determines what my ownership will be after future funding. If I own 20% at $5M post-money, and the next round values the company at $10M post-money, I'm diluted—my 20% is worth less proportionally.

**In practice:** Most seed rounds are quoted as post-money SAFEs now (valuation caps), so the pre/post distinction is less relevant than it was with equity rounds. But for equity deals, it's critical.

Here's what I learned from Flint: We raised at a $2.127M post-money valuation. If we'd done follow-on funding at a down round (valuation lower), my dilution would have been severe. Understanding post-money upfront helps you model worst-case scenarios.

The reason founders care: If you raise $1M at $10M pre-money, you give up 10% equity. If you raise the same $1M at $9M pre-money, you only give up 10% of $10M post, which is still 10%, so the math cancels. But psychologically and in negotiation, pre-money framing is cleaner.

**Bottom line:** Pre-money is the negotiation number. Post-money is the accounting number. For any term sheet, calculate both and make sure you understand ownership impact across future rounds.

### Q18.2: Difference between SAFE and convertible note? When do you recommend each?

**SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity):** No interest, no maturity date. It converts to equity in a future "qualifying event" (usually Series A). You own a percentage based on a valuation cap and discount.

**Convertible Note:** Actually a debt instrument. Has interest (usually 5-10%), maturity date (2-5 years), and converts to equity. If the company never raises again, you have a debt claim.

**When I recommend SAFE:**
- Seed rounds, startup is 12-18 months from Series A
- Early-stage, high risk, you want alignment (founders benefit if valuation goes up)
- Standard Y Combinator-style velocity rounds
- Founder experience matters less—SAFE is simpler to negotiate

**When I recommend Convertible Note:**
- Founder has proven track record, you want downside protection
- Bridge financing—you know a large round is coming in 6-12 months
- International founders where legal clarity matters more
- You want explicit terms on interest and maturity if conversion doesn't happen

**My experience:** With Flint, we did a SAFE round. Fast, founder-friendly, didn't require legal gymnastics. Clean conversion into Series A.

The critical difference: SAFE is **pro-founder**, convertible is **pro-investor** on downside. A convertible note has interest accruing, so if the company raises Series A at a lower valuation, you made 5-10% on that interest. SAFEs don't give you that protection.

**In Korean market:** Most early seed rounds use SAFE equivalents now, but traditional Korean VC still prefers convertible notes because they want defined terms. My recommendation: Use SAFE for young founders (faster momentum), convertible if founder has multiple exits already (you know they'll execute the conversion).

**Red flag:** If a founder refuses SAFE and insists on aggressive terms on a convertible note, they're signaling they don't believe in their own execution timeline. That's a warning signal.

### Q18.3: What is a cap table and why is anti-dilution important?

**Cap table (capitalization table)** is the ownership record of the company. It shows who owns what percentage of equity—founders, employees (via stock options), investors, early advisors. Every investment round updates the cap table.

Example from Flint:
- Co-founders: 65% (split between me and co-founder)
- Seed investors: 20% ($212K from Strong Ventures and Fast Ventures)
- Employee option pool: 15% (reserved for hiring)

After a Series A raise, those percentages adjust downward (dilution), but your absolute ownership stays the same unless anti-dilution kicks in.

**Anti-dilution** protects you from massive valuation drops. Imagine: Flint raised at $2.127M post-money. If Series A came in at $1.5M post-money (down round), my 20% ownership would be worth significantly less. Anti-dilution says: "If the next round is at a lower price, I get more shares to maintain economic value."

**Two types:**

1. **Full ratchet:** If down round happens, you get as many shares as if you'd invested at the lower price. Very founder-unfriendly. I'd never agree to this.

2. **Weighted average:** Calculated as (pre-money valuation + investment already made) / (post-money valuation + investment already made). More balanced, common in seed rounds.

**Why it matters:** 
- Protects early investors from founders raising at unsustainably low prices
- But it's dilutive to founders—their ownership decreases more
- It signals negotiating power imbalance

**My view:** For a seed SAFE, anti-dilution is typically not relevant because SAFEs convert at valuation cap. For a convertible note or equity round, I'd negotiate weighted average anti-dilution, never full ratchet. Full ratchet is only for Series A after you have real proof of traction.

**In practice:** The most important thing is building a cap table you can understand quickly. If you're managing 30+ investors and multiple option grants, and you can't tell who owns what, you have a problem. I'd rather invest in a founder who maintains a simple cap table (clean investor base) than a complex one.

### Q18.4: Explain key SaaS metrics (CAC, LTV, MRR, ARR).

These are the heartbeat metrics for any software business. If you can't explain these, you shouldn't be raising.

**MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue):** All subscription revenue in a month. If you have 100 customers paying $100/month, MRR = $10,000. Dead simple. ARR is just MRR × 12.

**CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** Total sales and marketing spend / number of new customers acquired. If you spend $10,000 on sales and marketing in a month and get 10 customers, CAC = $1,000 per customer.

**LTV (Lifetime Value):** Revenue you expect from a customer over their entire relationship. If average customer pays $100/month and stays for 24 months before churning, LTV = $2,400 (ignoring churn rate for simplicity).

**The magic number:** LTV / CAC should be at least 3:1. If your CAC is $1,000 and LTV is $2,400, you're breaking even in 4 months of relationship, and then profiting for the remaining 20 months.

**What founders get wrong:**
- They calculate LTV without accounting for churn. If 50% of customers churn after 6 months, your real LTV is half what you calculated.
- They don't separate CAC by channel. Your organic CAC might be $300, paid CAC might be $2,000. You need both numbers.
- They assume MRR will grow linearly. Retention and churn matter more than acquisition growth.

**From Flint:** We had decent MRR growth early (860 users in 7 months), but our UX friction meant CAC was high and churn was higher. Our LTV/CAC ratio wasn't healthy. That's partly why we pivoted. We were acquiring users inefficiently because the product experience was weak.

**From DUG:** We built a CS RAG system that cut support response time 50%. That reduced our support costs, which reduced CAC-equivalent (customer success requires less manual work). Better metrics.

**For investors:** I look at:
1. **CAC payback period:** How many months until a customer pays back their acquisition cost? Under 12 months is healthy, under 6 months is great.
2. **Churn rate:** Monthly churn under 5% is acceptable for B2B SaaS. If it's 10%+, retention is the problem, not growth.
3. **Net revenue retention (NRR):** Do your existing customers spend more over time (expansion revenue) or less (churn outweighs growth)? NRR above 120% is exceptional.

**Red flag:** Founder can't articulate their CAC and LTV. That means they're not analyzing their business with data.

### Q18.5: Fundamental difference between VC and PE?

**VC invests in early-stage, high-risk, high-growth companies.** We expect 90% to fail. The 10% that succeed need to return 100x because they're funding the losses of the other 90%.

**PE invests in mature, profitable companies with predictable cash flow.** They're buying cash flow. Returns are expected to be 3-5x over 5-7 years.

**Timeline:** VC is 7-10 year hold, PE is 3-5 year hold.

**How they make money:**

- **VC:** Takes 2% management fee + 20% carry (profit share). Flint raised $212K. If that investment became a $100M exit 10 years later, the carry would be enormous.
- **PE:** Takes 2% management fee + 20% carry, but the base investment is much larger. A PE firm might invest $500M in one company. The carry is enormous in absolute dollars.

**Investment thesis:**

- **VC:** "Can this company become a unicorn? Is the market big enough? Is the founder world-class?" The risk is existential.
- **PE:** "Can we improve operational efficiency? Can we consolidate competitors? Can we raise the revenue multiple?" The risk is margin compression.

**Management involvement:**

- **VC:** Hands-off comparatively. We provide strategic advice, connections, but founders run the show. If we micro-manage, the company dies.
- **PE:** Very involved. PE firms install new management, change operational systems, restructure debt. They're operational partners.

**Exit strategy:**

- **VC:** We want IPO or acquisition by a larger company. Ideally IPO because that's the largest return.
- **PE:** We want strategic sale or dividend recapitalization (paying ourselves out of the company's cash flow while still owning it).

**Why this matters for founders:** If you're taking VC funding, expect us to push for growth at all costs early on. Profitability is secondary. If you're taking PE funding, they'll immediately push to cut costs and optimize operations. Different playbooks.

**My perspective:** VC is more interesting than PE for me personally because it aligns with how I think—big vision, willingness to fail, moving fast. But I respect PE's discipline. The best VCs steal PE's rigor on unit economics.

### Q18.6: Define TAM, SAM, SOM and how you use them in investment decisions.

**TAM (Total Addressable Market):** The total potential revenue if you captured every possible customer in your market segment.

**SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market):** The portion of TAM you can actually reach. Geography, customer type, product limitations all reduce SAM.

**SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market):** The portion of SAM you can realistically capture in 3-5 years given competition and your execution capability.

**Example:** 

**TAM** = Global customer support software market = $30 billion/year

**SAM** = North American + European mid-market customer support = $8 billion/year (you can't service enterprise, too capital intensive)

**SOM** = Your realistic capture in 5 years = 2% of SAM = $160 million in annual revenue

**How I use this in investment decisions:**

1. **TAM viability:** If TAM is less than $500 million, I'm cautious unless there's path to expand. Small markets mean small exits. Flint's initial TAM (insurance-specific knowledge graphs) was probably $100-200M globally. That was constraining for growth.

2. **Founder honesty:** Founders always overshoot TAM. They'll say "the entire enterprise software market is our TAM ($100B+)" when really their SAM is $2B. I trust founders who break it down methodically, not ones throwing large numbers.

3. **Market expansion opportunity:** Does the company start in a small TAM but have a path to expand? DUG started in gaming (AR/VR), but the technology applies to enterprise training, medical visualization, etc. That's SOM expansion.

4. **Unit economics extrapolation:** If I know SOM and I know unit economics, I can project revenue potential. If SOM is $160M and my gross margin is 70%, I'm targeting $112M in gross profit. That determines fund size necessary to capture the market.

**The critical question:** Can this company realistically own 10-15% of their SOM in 5 years? If yes, the unit economics probably work. If SOM is too small for that to matter, pass.

**Red flag:** A founder who says "our TAM is unlimited" or who hasn't thought through geographic/customer constraints. That signals they're building without focus.

**My thesis:** The best businesses are in TAMs of $5-50 billion that are growing. SAM of $500 million to $2 billion is sweet spot for seed/Series A. SOM of $50-200M in 5 years is realistic.

- Liquidation preferences, anti-dilution, board seat
- Any special terms

**Total length:** 8-10 pages, maximum. More than that means you don't have conviction.

**Format I use:**
- Executive summary at the top
- Data points (metrics, financials) in tables, not paragraphs
- Competitive analysis in a simple 2x2 matrix
- My personal section highlighting "DJ's take" separately from objective facts

**What I never include:**
- Long company pitch decks reproduced in the memo (reference them, don't copy them)
- Flowcharts of organizational structure (nobody cares at seed stage)
- Detailed financial projections beyond 3 years (too speculative)

**The real test:** Could another partner read this memo in 10 minutes and make a decision to diligence further? If not, I've buried the key information.

### Q19.5: Define your personal investment thesis in one sentence.

**"I invest in founders who combine deep domain expertise with AI execution capability, targeting B2B operational efficiency in industries overlooked by venture capital, because the TAM is massive, unit economics are clear, and the founders who understand both the problem AND the technology will build the next $1B+ companies."**

Unpacked:

- **"Founders who combine..."** — This filters for execution ability. Domain expertise without technical skill = slow iteration. Technical skill without domain expertise = building for the wrong problem (Flint).

- **"...deep domain expertise..."** — I've learned that founders who've spent 5+ years in an industry understand customer pain points that 10 customer interviews can't reveal. This is unfair advantage.

- **"...with AI execution capability"** — Not just "using AI" but actually understanding when and how to apply it. CS RAG, knowledge graphs, predictive models—these need both vision and implementation.

- **"...targeting B2B operational efficiency..."** — Not consumer, not pure research, but businesses that need to work better. This narrows risk significantly.

- **"...in industries overlooked by venture capital"** — Huge markets (logistics, manufacturing, insurance, supply chain) are "boring" to VCs who chase sexy consumer apps. This is where returns hide.

- **"...TAM is massive..."** — I need to believe you can build a $100M+ company. Small TAMs produce small exits.

- **"...unit economics are clear"** — No Black box models. If I can't understand how you make money, I can't help you scale it.

- **"...will build the next $1B+ companies."** — Aspirational but clear target. I'm not looking for single-exit 5x returns, I'm looking for venture-scale outcomes.

**Why this thesis:**

This is based on what I've learned from Flint (domain + market mattered) and DUG (technical execution + speed matters). It also aligns with Altos' portfolio (Toss, Woowa, Karrot all have clear unit economics and domain expertise). And it's narrow enough to give me conviction to say "yes" and "no" decisively.

### Q19.6: Analyze a recently failed startup — why did it fail and when could you have known?

**I'll analyze Flint's pivot/failure path publicly:**

**Why Flint failed:**

**1. Built product before deeply understanding customer workflow.** We saw the problem (knowledge graphs for insurance) but underestimated the adoption friction. Insurance companies manage risk aversely—they want proven solutions, not experimental. We thought "better UX" would drive adoption, but the real issue was institutional inertia.

**When I should have known:** Q2-Q3 of Flint, when customer acquisition slowed. We'd hit early adopters (860 users in 7 months), but conversion from trial to paid was weak. Red flag.

**2. Prioritized growth over retention.** We celebrated 860 users but didn't look at weekly active users or feature retention. Typical startup mistake. If I'd demanded a CAC breakdown and LTV calculation early, we'd have seen our payback period was 18+ months—unsustainable.

**When I should have known:** When I first looked at cohort analysis. Each cohort after the first 100 users had lower retention. We were acquiring, not retaining.

**3. Pivot to insurance B2B was reactive, not proactive.** After B2C (knowledge graphs for consumers) failed quietly, we pivoted to insurance thinking domain expertise + enterprise sales would work. But pivoting into insurance when you don't have insurance industry relationships is slow. We were starting over in sales.

**When I should have known:** Before the pivot. We should have tested insurance market while B2C was running. Instead, we pivoted after momentum was lost.

**4. Ran out of capital before achieving product-market fit.** We raised $212K, burned it efficiently, but $212K isn't enough for a 18-24 month runway to find product-market fit in an enterprise sale cycle.

**When I should have known:** Day 1 of fundraising. With a $2.127M post-money valuation and limited runway, we were set up for constraint. We either needed to raise more (dilutive) or move faster (impossible given our GTM was slow).

**5. Co-founder misalignment on direction.** Not public, but our disagreement on "should we stay B2C with better UX" vs. "pivot to B2B" created friction. We did disagree but commit, but the commitment wasn't whole-hearted.

**When I should have known:** When the disagreement was unresolved. Founders having different visions on pivot = execution friction = slowness = failure.

**Lessons I learned:**

1. **Retention > Growth.** If you can't keep users, growth is just noise.
2. **Domain expertise matters before market entry.** Should have hired someone with insurance relationships.
3. **Runway math is brutal.** Should have raised $500K-1M, not $212K.
4. **Founder alignment on direction is non-negotiable.** Should have explicitly aligned on whether we were building B2C or B2B.
5. **Unit economics must be positive early.** By month 3, I should have known our CAC/LTV ratio.

**What would have saved Flint:**
- Deeper customer research before product build (talking to 50+ insurance companies before coding)
- Ruthless pivot earlier (by month 4, not month 8)
- Bigger seed round ($500K) with 24-month runway
- Clear co-founder alignment on domain we'd own

**When I'd have passed in a VC lens:** If I'd seen "860 users, zero revenue, pivoting to new market, unclear retention" in a diligence memo, I'd pass. Not because the founders are bad (they're excellent), but because the risk/reward was asymmetric.

### Q20.2: How do you deliver a rejection call to a founder?

**I do it fast, direct, and respectful. Here's my process:**

**Before the call:**
- I make sure I have a clear reason for the pass
- I'm not passive-aggressive ("Let me think about it") — if I'm passing, the founder knows
- I write down 2-3 specific reasons

**During the call (10-15 minutes):**

**Opening (1 minute):**
"Hey, I wanted to call because I've finished my diligence on [company]. I've decided to pass, and I want to be direct about why so you can get valuable feedback."

Why this works: Founder immediately knows the outcome. No suspense.

**The reason (3-5 minutes):**
I give ONE primary reason, not a laundry list. If I say "Your product needs work, your market is too small, your team is inexperienced, and your CAC is terrible," I've paralyzed them. Pick the real blocker.

Example: "I think the market opportunity is smaller than you're targeting. Insurance claims automation is a $50B TAM globally, but you're going after a subtype that's probably $5-10B. For me to do $100M+ in returns, I need bigger TAM conviction. That's on me, not you."

Why this works: Founder can't argue because I'm being specific and grounded.

**For things that are in their control, I'm constructive:**

"Your team is strong, but you're missing a sales person with enterprise experience. Most founders at your stage think they can sell to enterprises themselves, but enterprise sales cycles need relationship credibility. Get that hire done and you'll raise next round easily."

Why this works: Founder has action items. They don't feel helpless.

**What I avoid:**
- "I'll keep an eye on you" (false hope)
- Fake compliments ("Great pitch deck!") when the issue is deeper
- Vagueness ("Timing doesn't feel right" — what does that mean?)
- Long pauses that suggest I'm unsure

**Closing (1-2 minutes):**
"Here's how I can help: [I'll intro you to X who's investing in your space] [I can connect you with customers for feedback] [Let's catch up in 6 months when you have new metrics]."

Why this works: I'm ending on something constructive, not dead silence.

**After the call:**
- If I offered intros, I do them within 48 hours
- If I said I'd follow up in 6 months, I calendar it
- I send a brief email recap so there's no ambiguity

**Special cases:**

**"You're too early" rejection:**
"I think you need 12 more months of validation. Get to $50K MRR or 50 enterprise pilots, then I'm an easy yes. You'll be a better company and fundraising will be easier. Call me when you hit that."

**"Wrong fit for me" rejection:**
"This is a strong company, but my thesis is B2B operational software, and yours is B2C marketplace. I'd be a bad investor for you. Let me intro you to [VCs who focus on B2C]."

**"Founder/market/product issue" rejection:**
I'll be direct but private: "I have concerns about the direction here, and I don't want to be an investor through a pivot. But you're founders I'd bet on eventually."

**Why this matters:**

A rejection call done right:
- Leaves the door open for future raises
- Builds your reputation (founders will recommend you to others)
- Eliminates back-and-forth anxiety
- Gives founders actionable feedback

A rejection done poorly:
- Founders ghost you for months wondering what happened
- They'll bad-mouth you to other founders
- You'll get dragged into endless "but what if" conversations

**Honest take:** Some VCs avoid rejection calls. They just let diligence drag on indefinitely. That's the worst signal to a founder. I'd rather be rejected in 2 weeks than ghosted for 2 months.

### Q20.3: What's important when considering co-investment?

**Co-investment means splitting a check with other VCs. It's important for several reasons:**

**Strategic co-investors (most important):**

Not every co-investor is equal. I prefer co-investors who add value:
- **Sequoia Korea, Spark Capital, Corace:** These bring operational expertise
- **Domain-specific VCs:** If I'm investing in AI for manufacturing, I want a co-investor who's invested in 5 manufacturing companies
- **Founder networks:** A co-investor who can open doors in the US market is better than another Korea-focused fund

Why this matters: Co-investors I actually talk to are useful. Co-investors who disappear after the check clears add zero value.

**The portfolio overlap risk:**

If my co-investor is also leading Series A in a company that competes with my portfolio company, we have a conflict. I need to understand where they're investing broadly. If everyone I co-invest with is chasing the same "hot" sector, I've lost filtering discipline.

**Financial alignment:**

The best co-investments happen when everyone is aligned on:
- **Valuation:** If I think $2M post is fair and they want $3M, tension emerges later
- **Pro-rata rights:** Do they have the right to follow on Series A? If so, do I?
- **Board seat:** Who takes it? If both of us want a board seat, we fight in meetings
- **Control clauses:** Do we agree on governance? What happens if the company needs restructuring?

**Practical example from DUG:**

We had interest from multiple VCs. I wanted co-investors who had:
1. XR/gaming expertise (not generic investors)
2. Asia connections (for Indian, Southeast Asian markets)
3. Enough capital for Series A follow-on

An investor with none of those traits doesn't add value, even if they bring capital.

**How I evaluate co-investors:**

- **Track record:** How many Series A/B leads have they done? Do companies succeed?
- **Founder recommendations:** Ask portfolio founders, "Do you want them in future rounds?"
- **Communication style:** Do they ask good questions or just check boxes?
- **Speed:** Can they move fast or do they need committee approval?

**Red flags in co-investors:**

- Takes a board seat but never shows up
- Suddenly appears asking for data when a company struggles
- Won't commit to pro-rata for Series A
- Pressures the company to pivot toward their portfolio synergies

**What I look for:**

1. **Active co-investors** who'll help with customer intros, hiring, partnerships
2. **Syndicate alignment:** We believe in the same thesis, not just the same company
3. **Founder friendly:** Co-investor who trusts the founder vs. second-guessing
4. **Patient capital:** Understands the 10-year journey, not expecting returns in 3 years

**Practical structure I prefer:**

- **Altos as lead** (they bring Toss, Woowa expertise), **me as co-investor**
- **Everyone gets pro-rata rights** but it's defined upfront
- **One board seat total** (usually goes to the lead investor)
- **Monthly check-in calls** between co-investors, not just at board meetings

**What I avoid:**

- Co-investing with VCs I haven't worked with before (too much risk)
- Syndicates larger than 3-4 investors (governance becomes chaos)
- Situations where a co-investor has competing portfolio companies

**Why this matters for Altos:**

Altos has strong co-investment relationships (Sequoia, Bessemer, Greylock). That's part of why they're successful. I want to inherit and strengthen those relationships, not create new conflicts.

### Q20.4: How do you monitor portfolio company performance post-investment?

**I set up a structured monitoring system from day 1:**

**Monthly metrics dashboard (required):**

Every founder sends me a 1-pager by the 5th of the month:
- MRR/ARR and growth rate (% month-over-month)
- CAC, LTV, retention rate
- Cash burn rate and runway in months
- Product releases / key updates

Why monthly: Early-stage companies move fast. Quarterly is too slow to catch problems.

**Red flags I watch for:**

1. **Metrics flattening for 2+ months** → Customer acquisition problem or retention cliff
2. **Burn acceleration without revenue growth** → Spending recklessly
3. **Declining retention** → Product-market fit eroding
4. **Founder communication sporadic** → Possible team/morale issue
5. **CAC increasing, LTV flat** → Unit economics deteriorating

**Quarterly deep-dives (60 minutes per company):**

- Review last 3 months of metrics
- What surprised you? (Good and bad)
- What's your biggest blocker right now?
- Do you need help with hiring/intros/strategy?
- What do you need from me?

Why this works: I'm not micro-managing, but I'm staying informed. Founders feel supported, not interrogated.

**Board meetings (if I have a seat):**

- Monthly metrics review (15 min)
- Strategic discussion (45 min)
- Founder concerns/requests (30 min)

I use these to catch problems early.

**Portfolio company peer networks:**

I introduce portfolio founders to each other. Example: "You both are using Pinecone + Gemini, you should share learnings." This creates mutual accountability and learning.

**Annual founder summit (half-day):**

I bring all portfolio companies together:
- Share metrics learnings (anonymized)
- Customer intros between companies
- Hiring/partnership opportunities

Why: Reduces isolation and creates peer accountability.

**What I specifically monitor by stage:**

**Seed stage (first 6 months):**
- Weekly check-ins if there's a clear problem
- Focus: product-market fit signals
- Key metric: customer retention

**Series A (months 6-18):**
- Monthly metrics + monthly calls
- Focus: repeatable sales model
- Key metric: CAC payback period

**Series B+ (post-Series B):**
- Board meetings (typically monthly or quarterly)
- Focus: unit economics + market expansion
- Key metric: CAC + LTV efficiency

**How I intervene:**

I don't step in unless one of these conditions hits:
1. **Metrics are deteriorating 2+ months in a row** and founder hasn't acknowledged it
2. **Burn rate is unsustainable** and founder hasn't adjusted
3. **Founder is acting erratic** (missing calls, being defensive)
4. **Team is fragmenting** (key departures I didn't know about)

When I do intervene, it's collaborative: "I'm noticing X. What's your read on it? How can I help?"

**What I don't do:**
- Second-guess every decision
- Pressure founders to optimize purely for growth
- Require board seats if I don't need one
- Demand financial audits or legal reviews (that's on them unless it's serious)

**From Flint's experience:**

My biggest regret is not pushing harder on retention metrics earlier. We had user growth, but retention was weak. By the time I realized it, momentum was lost. Now I lead with retention questions.

**From DUG's experience:**

Strong founder communication made monitoring easy. Every month I knew exactly where we stood, and there were no surprises. I actually spent less time on monitoring with DUG than companies that hide metrics.

**My philosophy:**

Monitoring isn't about control, it's about early warning. The goal is to catch problems at month 2, not month 6. And to help. If a founder's revenue flattened, I know 20 other founders who hit that wall—let me connect them. If hiring is hard, I have connections. My job is to make the founder's job easier.

### Q20.5: What criteria do you use for follow-on investment decisions?

**I evaluate follow-on rounds with a different lens than initial investment. Higher bar.**

**The core question:** Would I invest in this company today at this valuation if I had no prior investment?

If the answer is no, I probably don't follow on.

**Specific criteria:**

**1. Growth trajectory (most important):**
- Is the company tracking toward $1M ARR at Series A? (or $10M at Series B?)
- If growth has decelerated, why? (Market saturation, execution slowdown, product pivot?)
- Bonus: Are they growing faster than comparable companies at their stage?

Example: DUG hit 55% YoY growth + major partnerships (Apple Vision Pro, Meta Quest+). That's follow-on worthy. If growth had stalled at 20% YoY, I'd be skeptical.

**2. Unit economics improvement:**
- CAC staying flat or decreasing? (Good)
- LTV increasing? (Good)
- Payback period under 12 months? (Good)
- Gross margin above 70%? (Good for SaaS)

If unit economics are deteriorating (CAC up, LTV down), the company is optimizing the wrong thing.

**3. Market validation beyond early adopters:**
- Do you have 5+ reference customers or 100+ paying customers?
- Are customers using the product regularly or churning fast?
- Net revenue retention above 100%? (Expansion revenue > churn)

If you only have 10 customers in Series A, you haven't validated the market.

**4. Founder + team execution:**

- Is the founding team intact? (Departures are bad signals)
- Are they hiring quality people? (Can they attract talent?)
- Do they have domain expertise or are they learning? (Experienced teams execute faster)

If the core team fragments between rounds, execution risk spikes.

**5. Competitive positioning:**
- Has competition emerged? If yes, why do you still believe in their moat?
- Are they losing customers to competitors or winning?
- Is the feature gap widening or narrowing?

Honest answer here: If competitors emerged and your company slowed, that's a pass.

**6. Narrative clarity:**
- Can the founder articulate why Series A is the right moment? (Not just "we need money")
- Is there a clear Series B path? (What are the milestones?)
- Do I believe the vision is still the same or have they pivoted too much?

Too many pivots = lack of conviction.

**What I explicitly DON'T prioritize for follow-on:**

- **Vanity metrics** (app downloads, free users, press coverage)
- **Team size** (doesn't matter if they're unproductive)
- **VC brand in the round** (Sequoia leading Series A is nice, but doesn't change unit economics)
- **Burn rate as a standalone metric** (Burn is fine if growth justifies it)

**Follow-on decision framework:**

I rank each company on:
- Growth (0-10)
- Unit economics (0-10)
- Market validation (0-10)
- Team strength (0-10)
- Competition/moat (0-10)

If average is 7+, I follow on. Below 6, I pass. 6-7 is where I dig deeper.

**How I communicate follow-on decisions:**

**If I'm following on:** "Here's why I'm excited to deploy capital again. Here's what I expect to see in Series B."

**If I'm passing:** "I have concerns about [specific metric]. Here's what I'd need to see to change my mind. Let me know how it develops."

**Special case: Down round or flat round:**

This is tricky. If a company raised at $5M pre and Series A comes in at $4M pre (down round), I have to ask: "Why is growth insufficient at this valuation?" 

Sometimes legitimate answers: Market adjusted, competition appeared, external macro shift. Sometimes bad answers: Team mismanagement, wrong GTM, lost product focus.

I'll follow on a down round if:
1. Founder is brutally honest about what went wrong
2. Clear plan to fix it
3. Unit economics are still solid (they're just scaling slower)
4. Founder isn't panicking

**My default: Wait and see.**

If I'm unsure about following on, I wait 6 months more metrics. Early follow-on commitment (pre-Series A) is rare for me.

### Q20.6: How do you think about exit strategy? When should you start preparing?

**Most VCs don't talk about exits early enough. I start thinking about it at investment.**

**Three exit types:**

1. **Strategic acquisition** (50% of outcomes)
   - Large company buys you to kill, integrate, or accelerate
   - Toss was eventually acquired (or merged with Bank Salad) to consolidate fintech
   - Exits usually at $100M-$1B+ valuation

2. **IPO** (30% of outcomes)
   - Company goes public, founders and investors get liquidity
   - Requires $100M+ ARR, 3+ year path to profitability
   - Rare but highest returns (if executed well)

3. **Secondary sale or dividend recapitalization** (20% of outcomes)
   - Company stays private but sells shares to secondaries
   - Or takes debt and pays out equity holders
   - Usually happens when IPO isn't imminent

**When to start preparing for exit:**

**From day 1:** Think about what acquirers exist.
- Toss: Who would want fintech? Incumbent banks, payment networks (Visa/Mastercard), regional players
- Flint: Who wants knowledge graphs? Insurance companies, consulting firms, enterprise software companies
- DUG: Who wants gaming + XR tech? Sony, Microsoft, Meta, Netflix

If you can't name 5 acquirers, your TAM might be too niche.

**Series A (18 months post-seed):** Articulate a clear 5-year vision.
- Not just "become a unicorn," but specific: "Become the dominant player in insurance claims automation in Asia"
- This vision should attract acquirers and public market investors

**Series B+:** Create actual exit optionality.
- Build relationships with potential acquirers
- Create 2-3 different paths (IPO track, strategic acquisition track, continue growth)
- At this stage, founders start thinking about it explicitly

**How I guide founders on exit thinking:**

**IPO path (high bar):**
- Requires: $50M+ ARR, clear path to profitability, 3-4 year runway
- Typical timeline: 8-10 years from seed
- Founders need public market experience or advisors who do

**Strategic acquisition path (more common):**
- Requires: $5-50M ARR, clear strategic value to acquirer
- Typical timeline: 5-7 years from seed
- Founders should build relationships with target acquirers during growth phase

**Secondary/dividend recapitalization:**
- Requires: Profitable or close to it
- Good for: Founders who want partial liquidity before full exit
- Typical timeline: Can happen at any stage, but usually years 5+

**What I tell founders:**

"In Series A, I want to see you thinking about how this company becomes strategic to [Toss competitor, Meta, Microsoft]. Not obsessively, but enough to guide product strategy."

Why? Because the best exits often happen when the company is so differentiated that one buyer MUST have them. That's strategic value.

**Red flags for exit potential:**

- **No clear acquirers:** "Who would buy this?" is a question I ask loudly
- **Founder wants IPO but company is 5 years from profitability:** Unlikely path
- **Product is too specific to one market:** Limits strategic value
- **Founder doesn't care about exit strategy:** Usually a sign they're not thinking operationally

**How exits affect investment strategy:**

This informs my follow-on decisions. If a company is on track for strategic acquisition at $100M+ valuation, I'll follow on aggressively. If the exit path is unclear and valuation is rising, I'm cautious.

**Timeline I use:**

- **Years 0-2:** Product-market fit, seed/Series A
- **Years 2-4:** Scaling, Series B/C, achieving $5-20M ARR
- **Years 4-6:** Market dominance, preparing exit conversations
- **Years 6-8:** Negotiating exit or going IPO

**Special case: Secondary sales:**

I'm increasingly comfortable with secondaries (selling some shares to late-stage capital) because:
- Founders get partial liquidity
- Company stays independent longer
- I can redeploy capital to new investments

Toss likely did secondaries before exit because the IPO/acquisition timeline extended.

**My personal approach at Altos:**

I'm thinking about exits from the moment I invest. This shapes:
- Who I bring as co-investors (strategic relationships)
- How I communicate with founders (exit clarity)
- When I push for specific milestones (Series B requirements)

A founder once told me, "You're always thinking 5 years ahead." That's the job. You can't maximize exits if you're only focused on next quarter's metrics.

### Q20.7: When evaluating a founder in a meeting, what do you look at first?

**In order of importance:**

**1. How they explain the problem (first 2 minutes):**

Do they describe the problem from the customer's perspective or from their own perspective?

Bad: "We're building an AI system for insurance claims. The market is $50B. AI is the future."
Good: "Insurance companies spend 30% of claims processing cost on manual document review. Our automation cuts that to 5%."

The second is specific to a customer pain point with economics. The first is generic pitch speak.

I'm assessing: Does this founder really understand the customer or are they selling a solution?

**2. Conviction level (minute 2-4):**

Do they hesitate or equivocate when I push back?

Bad: Me: "What if someone else builds this?" Them: "Oh, that's possible. We'd pivot then."
Good: Me: "What if someone else builds this?" Them: "They won't have [specific advantage]. Here's why..." OR "That would be fine, we'd just compete on X."

The second shows conviction without arrogance. The first shows weakness.

I'm assessing: Will this founder persist when things get hard, or will they fold at first real obstacle?

**3. Self-awareness and honesty (minute 4-6):**

Do they acknowledge risks or weaknesses?

Bad: "We have no weaknesses. We're perfectly positioned. This is a slam dunk."
Good: "Our biggest risk is enterprise sales velocity. None of us have done enterprise sales before. Here's how we're mitigating it..."

Founders who acknowledge weaknesses are more credible. They're thinking about reality, not selling me a fantasy.

I'm assessing: Are they delusional or grounded? Will I be able to help them through hard parts, or will they deny there's a problem?

**4. How they answer questions I ask (minute 6-20):**

Do they actually listen to my question or do they give their prepared answer?

Bad: Me: "Who's your biggest competitor?" Them: [launches into prepared pitch deck content about market size]
Good: Me: "Who's your biggest competitor?" Them: "Directly, Company X. We differentiate because [specific reason]. But honestly, the bigger risk is market adoption timing, not direct competition."

The first signals they're not listening or they're rigid. The second shows intellectual flexibility and analytical thinking.

I'm assessing: Can I have a real conversation with this founder, or will every answer be a canned pitch?

**5. How they talk about their team (minute 20-25):**

Do they describe team members as equals with specific strengths, or do they subordinate them?

Bad: "My co-founder is great, does engineering stuff. We have a few people."
Good: "My co-founder leads product design and has 10 years experience in this industry. She's incredible at identifying the actual user need, which is why I brought her on. We have a head of sales who came from [competitor], brings 8 relationships already."

The second shows founder humility and clear roles. The first suggests unclear decision-making.

I'm assessing: Is this a team I believe can execute, or a founder with a weak support system?

**6. Energy and body language (throughout):**

Are they energized when talking about the problem, or just the funding/exit?

Red flags:
- Constantly checking phone/watch
- Defensive when questioned
- Overly polished (every answer sounds rehearsed)
- No genuine emotion when describing customer impact

Green flags:
- Leans forward when explaining problem
- Gets animated when talking about customers, not when talking about valuation
- Listens intently to my questions, takes notes
- Shows vulnerability (admits uncertainty about specific things)

I'm assessing: Do they love building this specific company, or do they love the idea of being a founder?

**What I DON'T focus on:**

- **Their pitch deck quality** (irrelevant if the idea is good)
- **Their background pedigree** (Stanford vs. state school doesn't matter if they can execute)
- **Their verbal fluency** (some great founders are quiet)
- **Their age** (irrelevant, both young and old founders can succeed)
- **Their appearance** (shouldn't matter but biases exist)

**The "smell test":**

After 30 minutes with a founder, I have a gut feeling: "Do I believe this person can build a billion-dollar company?" Not "Is this company guaranteed to succeed" (no companies are), but "Does this founder have the qualities that enable success?"

If the answer is yes, I dig deeper. If the answer is no, I pass.

**Why this matters:**

Early-stage investing is 70% founder, 30% idea. A great founder with an okay idea will pivot to a great idea. A mediocre founder with a great idea will execute it poorly.

So in that meeting, I'm mostly assessing founder quality. The idea is the vehicle, but the founder is the driver.

**My final question (always asked):**

"If I told you right now that your specific product idea won't work, but the market you're targeting is huge and real, would you stay in this market and build something different, or would you move on to a new market?"

If they say "Stay and figure out the right solution," they're founder-quality. If they say "I'd move on," they're idea-quality, not founder-quality. I take option 1.

## SECTION 21: Decision-Making & Crisis Management

### Q21.1: A portfolio company calls with a crisis — what do you do in the first 5 minutes?

First, I pause everything and listen. The first 5 minutes is about understanding, not deciding. I ask three things: What happened exactly? What's the immediate threat to survival? What's been tried already?

I learned this at Flint when we had that UX friction problem. We panicked, pivoted too fast, and made it worse. Now I know that the worst thing you can do in the first 5 minutes is *decide*. You gather signal.

My process: (1) Confirm facts — separate what we know from what we're guessing. (2) Assess severity — is this existential or painful? (3) Understand constraints — what options are actually available right now? (4) Identify the decision-maker — is it the CEO? The technical lead? Me? Usually it's not me.

Then I say: "Here's what I heard. Here's what I'll do in the next 24 hours. You focus on [specific thing]. Don't make decisions while we're in shock."

At DUG, when the Meta funding fell through, I didn't react immediately. I called the team, confirmed everyone still believed in the vision, and then we reframed it as validation that we could build something world-class without them. That moment taught me: crisis isn't about speed. It's about clarity.

The CEO should drive the solution. My job is to expand the frame so they're not making decisions in the dark.

---

### Q21.2: Three crises happen simultaneously (customer churn, tech issue, team conflict) — how do you prioritize?

This actually happened at DUG. We had customer churn, an API infrastructure problem, and internal conflict about product direction. I didn't try to solve all three.

My priority: *Can we survive the next 7 days?* That's the filter.

If tech issue threatens survival (revenue goes to zero), that's first. If team conflict is so bad that key people are walking, that's first. If customer churn is slow-bleed, that's third.

At DUG: The tech issue could kill us in a week (contracts wouldn't renew if the product broke). Customer churn was happening but slower. Team conflict was real but not explosive. So: (1) Tech issue gets the best engineers immediately, (2) I personally owned customer conversations to stem churn, (3) Product and engineering leads met to align on direction — not to solve it perfectly, just to stop bleeding.

The mistake founders make is treating all three equally. They're not. One is the bleed that kills you fastest.

I also think about leverage: Who can solve this? If it requires the CEO, it's higher priority than if it requires middle management. If it requires external capital, it's higher priority than if it requires process change.

You also have to be honest: *Which one can we actually win?* At Flint, we tried to fix customer churn while pivoting product while raising money. We lost because we picked three battles we couldn't win simultaneously. At DUG, we picked *one* — building an undeniable product — and let the other stuff matter less for 6 months.

### Q21.3: How do you decide with incomplete information under time pressure?

I make two lists: *What do I know?* and *What am I guessing?* Then I move forward assuming everything on the guess list is wrong.

At TmaxTibero, we had incomplete telemetry on database performance. We needed to ship a fix, but we weren't sure which approach would work. So I asked: *If I'm wrong, what's the cost?* If the cost is "we lose a customer," that's unacceptable. If the cost is "we have to rollback in 24 hours," that's acceptable.

We went with the safer approach that we could reverse, got real data, then made the better decision.

Time pressure makes people emotional. I try to separate: *What do I need to know before I decide?* versus *What's just nice to know?* That distinction saves weeks.

At Flint, we had to decide whether to pivot or die. Incomplete info: we didn't know if our new direction would work. But we knew our current path was failing. So the decision was almost made for us — the cost of staying the course was higher than the cost of the unknown pivot.

I also use a framework: *What's reversible? What's not?* Reversible decisions can be made fast. Irreversible ones need more data.

Hiring a person? Hard to reverse. Speed of decision: slower. Technical stack? Medium reversibility. Speed: medium. Which customer segment to focus on? Pretty reversible. Speed: fast.

The best decision-makers I've met — like my CEOs — they're comfortable with 60% information if the reversibility is high. They don't chase 95% information on things they can fix later.

### Q21.4: When intuition and data conflict, which do you trust?

Data. Always data. But then I ask: *Is the data actually telling me something true, or just something measurable?*

At DUG, our data said: "Users love this feature." But my intuition said: "But they're not paying extra for it." So I trusted the data on what they *did*, and my intuition on what they *valued*. We built differently after that.

Intuition is really pattern recognition. It's my brain's way of saying: *I've seen something like this before and it usually ends badly.* That's useful. But it's not truth.

When I met the Altos team, something just felt right. But I didn't decide based on feeling. I looked at their check sizes, their companies' outcomes, their 10+ year horizon. The feeling was useful — it made me pay attention. The data was decisive.

At Flint, my intuition said: "We're building the future of AI." Data said: "Users are leaving because the UX is broken." I prioritized intuition. That was a mistake.

So my real answer: *I trust the conflict.* When they disagree, it means I'm missing something. At that point, I dig deeper. Usually the issue is that I'm measuring the wrong thing or not seeing the full picture.

The data tells you what happened. Intuition tells you what it means. Together, they're powerful.

### Q21.5: Tell me about a time your decision-making process was wrong.

Flint. We had strong product-market signal (860 users in 7 months, $2.1M post-money). We had good investors. But we had UX friction that was quietly killing retention.

My decision: *Commit harder. Build faster. Hire more engineers.*

The data I saw: growth metrics, user count, investor enthusiasm. The data I ignored: user interviews where people said "this is hard to use." I rationalized it as "early users are technical." They weren't — we just weren't listening.

What went wrong in my process: I was pattern-matching to "move fast." That works for some companies, some phases. But it doesn't work when the core problem is *how the product feels*, not *what it does*. I brought a scaling playbook to a product-quality problem.

The decision-making mistake wasn't the speed. It was that I stopped asking: *Are we winning for the right reasons?* I saw the metrics and moved into execution mode instead of diagnostics mode.

I think about it this way: If the only way we grow is by convincing engineers that our UX is good enough, we're not building a 10x company. I should have stepped back and said: *We have 6 months to radically improve this experience, or we have to pivot.* Instead, I said: *We have 6 months to prove our current direction works.* Very different questions.

The lesson I took to DUG: Metrics lie if you're not measuring the right thing. We obsessed about customer quality, not just customer count. About retention, not just activation. About partnerships, not just users. And when data conflicted with customer interviews, we dug in — not doubled down.

I don't think I'll make that exact mistake again, but I'm sure I'll make new ones in the same *category*: misreading what the data is actually telling me.

## SECTION 22: Ethics & Integrity

### Q22.1: A portfolio CEO is caught inflating numbers — how do you respond?

I report it. Immediately. To Altos management and the audit committee if one exists.

And then I meet with the CEO separately, not in a gotcha moment. I want to understand: Did they know? Are they under pressure? Were they panicking?

Here's the thing: *I'm not a police officer.* My job isn't to punish. But my job *is* to protect the fund and other investors. If a CEO is inflating numbers, it usually means they're in a failure spiral and making worse decisions to cover up the first problem.

At that point, the conversation isn't moral. It's practical: *This ends your ability to operate as CEO. The question now is: Do we fix it together, or do we bring in management?*

I've seen founders cave under pressure and make bad calls. Some of them are good operators who panicked once. Others are fundamentally dishonest. I need to know which.

But here's what I won't do: I won't cover it up to protect the fund's return. That's the opposite of integrity. Fudging numbers to protect a down round destroys everything downstream. It pollutes the market. It makes other founders think it's normal.

One thing I learned in Korea (where I grew up) and Germany (where I studied) is that trust is the only currency. Once you break it, money doesn't matter. You can't build great companies on dishonesty.

So: Report. Understand. Act decisively. If they're dishonest, get them out. If they panicked and we can fix it, we fix it together. But I never participate in the cover-up.

### Q22.2: A competing VC is chasing the same deal and you feel FOMO — what do you do?

I don't lead with FOMO. I lead with the question: *Do we actually want to own this?*

FOMO is a feeling. It's useful as a signal that something is happening. But it's a terrible decision-making framework.

At DUG, a couple of other VCs showed interest around the time we were diligencing. That was exciting. But I asked: *Do we believe in this team and market enough to own it if no one else is interested?* If the answer is no, then FOMO is just making a bad investment worse by adding pressure.

My actual process: (1) I do the diligence at my pace, not the market's pace. (2) If another VC is interested, great — it validates the market. It doesn't change the fundamentals. (3) I make a call on terms that reflect value, not panic. (4) If we lose the deal, we lose the deal.

I've also seen deals that look hot because of FOMO turn into mediocre investments. Too many cooks, misaligned incentives, and a team that's bounced between VCs trying to optimize terms instead of optimizing their product.

The hard part: *Saying no when the market says yes.* That takes confidence and conviction. But if Altos' edge is the 10+ year horizon and founder-centricity, then we're in the privileged position of *not* having to chase hot deals.

I'd rather miss 5 good deals than rush into 1 bad one. The opportunity cost of capital in a hot deal with mediocre fundamentals is brutal over a 10-year horizon.

A real friend understands. They might think it's boring, but they get it.

At Flint, we raised from strong angels. They became friends. But we also had institutional investors. The moment that institutional money came in, I couldn't casually share information with friends anymore. It changed the dynamic, but it was necessary.

This is actually a test of integrity that *matters*. Anyone can say they won't do something unethical. But can you actually say no to a friend? That's character.

### QA.5: (Warren Buffett) Has your integrity ever been tested?

Yes. At Flint, I had a choice between being honest (saying "this product has a fundamental UX problem") or being loyal to the team's vision ("let's double down and get faster").

I chose loyalty. I regret that. My integrity was tested and I failed. I prioritized harmony over honesty, and it cost us time and credibility.

Since then, I've learned: *Real integrity means being willing to say uncomfortable truths to people you care about.*

I've also been tested on money. At TmaxTibero, I was offered a significant bonus to stay and handle a messy transition. I considered it (I was not wealthy). Instead, I chose to leave so the company could bring in fresh leadership. That was integrity costing me money.

And yes, I've been tested on confidentiality. I've had friends ask me about companies and opportunities. I've said no every time. It costs relationship capital. But it's non-negotiable.

Integrity isn't tested when it's easy. It's tested when you lose something. I try to stay honest about when I pass those tests and when I don't.

### QA.6: (Tim Cook) Prove your curiosity.

I've studied in 4 countries because I wanted to understand different cultures' approaches to technology, education, and business.

I learned German to fluency not because it was required, but because I wanted to think in another language and understand how that changes what you notice.

At DUG, I spent time learning about gaming from the ground up — mechanics, economics, esports, streaming. Not because I had to, but because I wanted to actually *understand* the space, not just invest in it.

I ask founders questions that go deeper than their pitch. "Tell me about a customer you lost and why." "What would you do if your funding got cut in half?" "What's the one thing about your market that nobody agrees with?" I ask because I actually want to know, not because it's a checklist.

I read across disciplines. Right now I'm reading about attention economics, about AI infrastructure, about leadership philosophy. Not for work — because I'm genuinely curious.

Curiosity to me is *genuine not knowing* and *persistent asking* until I understand. Not asking to impress. Asking because you actually want to know.

### QA.7: (Ray Dalio) "Why" did you do that?

I use this framework all the time. For every decision, I try to answer: *Why?* Not once, but multiple times.

At DUG, we made a decision to focus on Apple and Meta partnerships. Why? Because we saw that gaming was becoming mobile-first and console-second. Why? Because our users were playing on those devices. Why? Because esports and streaming were moving to mobile. Why? Because attention is moving to mobile.

Each "why" is a test: *Is this reasoning solid or am I assuming?*

At Flint, when I said "let's keep executing," the real "why" was: "I'm scared to admit the product is broken, and the team is excited, so it's easier to commit." Not a good why.

At Altos, when I commit to an investment, I need to be able to answer "why" seven times and still believe it at the end. If I can't, I probably don't actually understand what I'm buying.

Most people stop at the first "why." They say "because the market is big" or "because the founder is great." Then you dig: "Really? *Why* are you sure?" And often the answer isn't there.

The discipline of asking "why" repeatedly is how you find your own bullshit.

### QA.8: (Rudy Mawer) Why should we NOT hire you?

Because I haven't been through a full VC cycle yet.

I haven't seen a Series A company I backed evolve through Series B, C, exit. I haven't managed through a real downturn with a portfolio. I haven't experienced the pressure of LP returns or the politics of a board room with multiple investors.

These are things you learn through time and iteration. I'm betting that my operational experience (3 different companies, different stages, different outcomes) gives me the foundation to learn VC fast. But it's still a bet.

Second: I might be impatient with slow-moving processes. I come from startups where things move fast. VC has different rhythms. I might chafe under that.

Third: I have blind spots. I don't know what I don't know about markets, geographies, or company types I haven't seen. I could miss an obvious pattern because I haven't been exposed to it.

And honestly: I could just not be good at this. Intelligence and work ethic don't guarantee success in VC. Some people have it, some don't. I think I do, but I'm not certain.

The best hire is someone who knows their weaknesses and is committed to fixing them. I know mine. I'm committed. But you should hire me *because* of that self-awareness, not despite it.
